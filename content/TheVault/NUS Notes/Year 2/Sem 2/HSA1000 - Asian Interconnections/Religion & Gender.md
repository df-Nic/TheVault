# Categories
---
They are <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>neutral basic facts</span></b> about a person/group/object, but this <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-red)'>might not be always true</span></b>.

> [!quote] Definition of category by the Cambridge dictionary
> (in a system for dividing things according to appearance, quality, etc.) a type, or a group of things having some features that are the same.

**Categories are social constructed**, with <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>different meanings and implications</span></b> across different societies.

Categories <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-green)'>can be helpful</span></b> but they <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-red)'>oversimplify the complex realities</span></b> of our everyday lives.

Practiced of **classifying and simplifying** are <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>practices of power</span></b> (*because they can make decisions*).

<b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>Ordinary people’s experiences</span></b> also contribute to their understandings of the categories.
# Gender
---
Are gender and sex the same thing. Both determines biological traits.

> [!quote] Oxford dictionary of human geography
> Gender is the <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>different roles, qualities, and responsibilities attributed</span> </b> to men and women in society.
> 
> Whereas <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>sex denotes biological differences between men and women</span></b>, **gender refers to socially constructed characteristics** that produce gender roles based on supposed differences that arise from sexuality and physiology.

There are **specific gender norms**, which are the <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>standards and expectations to which women and men generally conform</span></b>, within a range that defines a particular society, culture and community at that point in time.

> [!info] Gender as performative
> Gender: a social construction largely defined by what we do. A “doing” rather than a “being”.
# Religion
---
There are <span style='color:var(--mk-color-orange)'>2 categories in religion</span>:
1) **Religious**, a person has a <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>set of beliefs, symbols, and practices</span></b> (*for example rituals*), which is based on the idea of the sacred, and which unites believers into a socio-religious community.
2) **Secular**, which are people <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>not connected with religious or spiritual matters</span></b>. It tends to be associated with western modernisation.

However the <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>2 are not completely in seperate spheres</span></b>. The secular state has the function of constantly defining what can be accepted as religion and where its boundaries should properly be.

However bring <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>non-religious is not the same as secular</span></b> (*can still practice religious things*), though it is not clear cut (*Who draws the line is in power*).

## Turkey's Fashion Veiling

Firstly, <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>majority</span></b> of the people **identify as Muslims**. The females has a <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>set of dress codes for their headscarves</span></b> (*veiling*).

Usually women will be **covered from head to toe**, portraying that they are bring <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-red)'>oppressed</span></b>.

Dress codes and the related gender norms in urban **public spaces** reflect the Turkish state’s <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>emphases on secularism as political ideologies</span></b>.

<b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>Turkish Muslim women engage religion and gender as their practices</span></b>. Being religious, especially dressing for religious expressions, implies different meanings to individuals.

Thus they will have to <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>adjust their veiling based on the different public spaces</span></b>. This choices of clothing are affected by wider relations of power.
- The struggle between the secular state and resurgent Islamic politics
- Veiling being associated with lower class, less educated, and unprofessional (*class distinction*). Limited access to equal opportunities

> [!summary] However some view veiling as professionals
> Younger professionals in Turkey <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>view veiling as transgressive</span></b>. As they are challenging the Turkish secularist ideology on keeping religion in the private sphere.

Thus **women’s choices of fashion**: premised on <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>political, social, and economic consequences and embedded in secularist-dominant social norms</span></b> in Turkey.
## Gender & Religion as a Category in China

Since 1949, the Chinese Communist Party has <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>used gender to reinforce state ideologies</span></b> through defining acceptable gender norms (*Chinese used to prefer male over females*).

As for **religion**, the Chinese communist party <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>recognise different religions but are overseen</span></b>. Where they conduct activities must be in authorized that are registered within the state system.

Those who **practice outside** the state legal framing: considered <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-red)'>illegal and superstitious</span></b>.

Secularism as the fundamental of the CCP regime
- The secular state has <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>authorities over religious affairs</span></b> and communities
- <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>Loyalty to the state and the ruling party</span></b> as the premises of state approval (*Love your country, love your religion*)

All these norms are <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>decided by those in power</span></b>. And they <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-yellow)'>affect those who are in and out</span></b> of these groups.
### Veiling in China

In China, veiling is seen as a <b><span style='color:var(--mk-color-red)'>symbol of Islamic extremism</span></b>. The government has went on to:
- Campaigning Uyghur women to adopt modern aesthetics by removing face veils and jilbab
- Making rule applied to Muslim men on facial hair and clothing in public spaces
